# Experimental Results — Keyed Quantum States and Scrambling

**Project:** Quantum Cryptography Research: Keyed Quantum States and Scrambling  
**Implementation:** Python, Qiskit, NumPy; statevector-based simulation  
**Research status:** Exploratory, small-scale computational experiments; not a security proof  
**Evidence status (November 2026):** Numerical findings below are **previously recorded results**, not independently reproduced or verified during preparation of this summary. The published notebook has its execution outputs cleared. Re-run it to verify the values and archive the resulting outputs before citing them as reproduced findings.

## Research question

How do measurement access and publicly known scrambling circuits affect an attacker's ability to distinguish and recover keys in a four-qubit, 64-candidate toy quantum-state generator?

## Experimental setup

- **System:** 4 qubits; 6-bit candidate key (64 possible keys); target key `13`.
- **Baseline generator:** Key-dependent rotations and entangling gates; candidate states evaluated with Qiskit statevectors.
- **Attack models:** Ideal target-state fidelity (Experiment 2); finite simulated measurement samples in the Z basis (Experiment 3) or both Z and X bases (Experiments 4–5).
- **Public scrambling:** Repeated, key-independent rotation and entangling layers; depths `0, 1, 2, 4, 8` in the sweep.
- **Important distinction:** These are simulated measurements sampled from exact statevectors, **not runs on quantum hardware**. The attacker can compute candidate distributions from the known circuits.

### Experiment 1 — Keyed state generation

A four-qubit circuit encodes a six-bit candidate key using rotations and entangling gates. The recorded state fidelity between the states generated by keys `13` and `25` was **0.3125**. This shows those *two particular states* are not identical; it does not establish that every pair of keys is distinguishable.

### Experiment 2 — Exhaustive comparison with ideal state information

The attacker evaluated all **64 candidate keys** against the target using ideal statevector fidelity. The previously recorded ranking recovered **key 13**, with no other key reported to have fidelity approximately one. This is a powerful attacker model with ideal target-state information, not an attack using only a small number of physical copies.

### Experiment 3 — Finite Z-basis measurements

The notebook samples **100 Z-basis outcomes** and compares the observed distribution against predicted distributions for each key using squared error. The earlier reported outcome showed ambiguity between **keys 13 and 29**. This demonstrates a limitation of that particular measurement setting and finite sample, not a general impossibility of key recovery.

### Experiment 4 — Combining measurement bases

The attacker used **100 simulated measurements in Z and 100 in X**, ranking candidate keys by combined log likelihood. The earlier reported result identified **key 13**. Changing the measurement basis can expose information absent from a Z-only measurement distribution; the comparison also uses more total shots than Experiment 3.

### Experiment 5 — Public scrambling depth sweep

The notebook applies publicly specified, key-independent scrambling layers and tests recovery at depths **0, 1, 2, 4, and 8**, with **30 trials per depth** and **100 simulated shots per basis per trial**. The previously recorded result was:

| Scrambling depth | Recorded successful recoveries | Recorded success rate |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0 | 30 / 30 | 100% |
| 1 | 30 / 30 | 100% |
| 2 | 30 / 30 | 100% |
| 4 | 30 / 30 | 100% |
| 8 | 30 / 30 | 100% |
| **Total** | **150 / 150** | **100%** |

**Interpretation:** Under this toy-model attacker, the tested public scrambling layers did not stop recovery of the target key. These are recorded outcomes awaiting a reproducibility check, not evidence of a universal success rate or a cryptographic security claim.

### Experiment 6 — Reversibility of public scrambling

For key `13` at depth `4`, the notebook constructs the inverse of the public scrambling circuit and compares the recovered state to the original. The previously recorded recovery fidelity was approximately **1.0**, consistent with the reversibility of the known unitary operation. This is an expected property of a known unitary circuit, not a demonstration of breaking a secure cryptosystem.

## Limitations and interpretation

1. The model uses only **four qubits and 64 keys**, making exhaustive search practical; it is not a real-world cryptographic security benchmark.
2. The measurements are **statevector-simulated**. No hardware noise, device errors, or quantum-processor execution is included in these experiments.
3. Attackers are given the circuit family and can compute ideal candidate-state probabilities. Ideal-state access in Experiment 2 is stronger than finite-shot access.
4. The public scrambling layers are **key-independent** and reversible when known; their depth alone is not evidence of one-wayness.
5. The depth sweep targets the same secret key and uses a particular sampling setup; **150/150** should not be generalized to arbitrary keys, random seeds, larger systems, or stronger security claims.
6. The notebook currently stores **no execution outputs**, so the numbers above are **historically recorded observations**, not fresh independently reproduced results.
7. Experiment 7 (key-dependent scrambling) is **in progress** and intentionally excluded from the completed-results table.
